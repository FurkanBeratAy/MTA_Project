CUNY Bus Route Analysis

Strategic Implementation of Automated Camera Enforcement for Enhanced Student Transportation

Executive Summary: This comprehensive analysis examines NYC bus routes serving CUNY campuses to identify optimal candidates for Automated Camera Enforcement (ACE) implementation. Using rigorous machine learning models trained on real ACE performance data, we have identified priority routes demonstrating significant potential for speed improvements, directly addressing the critical issue that 65% of CUNY students experience class lateness due to transportation challenges.

Critical Reading Guide

  • What we know: Current speeds, violation patterns, student impact, and historical ACE selection patterns are well-documented
  • What we estimate: Future speed improvements based on route characteristics—these carry uncertainty and should not be treated as guaranteed
  • What we don't know: Enforcement capacity, street infrastructure, political feasibility, and behavioral adaptation will all affect real-world outcomes
  • Bottom line: This analysis identifies promising candidates for further investigation and pilots—not deployment decisions

Project Overview

Research Objective

Quantify the impact of Automated Camera Enforcement (ACE) implementation on CUNY student commute times and identify optimal routes for deployment to reduce bus lane violations and improve service reliability.

Data Sources

MTA Open Data with 3.4M speed records, NYC traffic violation database, comprehensive CUNY student survey with 185 validated responses across all boroughs.

Machine Learning

R² 0.948

Ridge Regression model trained on 571 routes to predict ACE effectiveness

Methodology & Approach

Data Collection and Processing

MTA Bus Open Data: Aggregated 3,411,549 speed records from 2025 and 68,716 baseline records (2015-2019) covering all NYC bus routes. Data includes segment-level speeds, operating times, and route characteristics.

ACE Implementation Records: Compiled database of 64 routes with documented ACE deployment dates from NYC DOT, providing ground truth for model training.

Student Survey: Stratified random sample of 185 CUNY students across 12 campuses, response rate 34%, margin of error ±7.2% at 95% confidence level. Survey instrument validated through pilot testing.

Machine Learning Pipeline

Feature Engineering: Created 20+ features including baseline_speed, route_number, route_type, borough encoding, campus indicators, days_since_ace, and interaction terms.

Data Preprocessing: StandardScaler normalization applied to all numeric features. Categorical variables encoded using one-hot encoding. Missing values handled through median imputation.

Model Training: 70/30 train-test split with stratification by ACE status. Hyperparameter tuning via grid search with 5-fold cross-validation.

Model Selection: Ridge Regression selected based on test R² (0.948), cross-validation stability (CV R² 0.919 ± 0.036), and interpretability of coefficients.

What this means for interpretation: Our model explains 94.8% of historical variation in which routes got ACE (R² = 0.948). This is excellent for describing historical patterns. But the remaining 5.2% represents real-world complexity we can't capture—political decisions, budget availability, enforcement readiness, etc. Any specific route prediction should be treated as "this route is a strong candidate" not "this will improve by exactly X mph."

Priority Routes for ACE Implementation

Machine Learning–Enhanced Prediction Methodology

To identify which routes would most benefit from Automated Camera Enforcement (ACE), we employed advanced machine learning techniques combined with comprehensive MTA datasets.

1. Data Integration & Feature Engineering

  • Integrated pre-ACE baseline speeds (2015–2019) with current 2025 segment speeds
  • Flagged routes with existing ACE implementation and CUNY campus service
  • Engineered features including days_since_ace, route type, borough encoding, and campus indicators
  • Calculated speed changes for routes with ACE to establish ground truth

2. Model Training & Evaluation

Compared four regression algorithms using rigorous evaluation:

  • Ridge Regression: R² = 0.948 (selected as best model)
  • Random Forest: R² = 0.896
  • Gradient Boosting: R² = 0.897
  • Lasso Regression: R² = 0.945

All models were trained on 80% of data with StandardScaler normalization and validated using 5-fold cross-validation to ensure robust performance.

3. Predicting ACE Benefits

The Ridge Regression model was used to simulate ACE implementation on all 529 routes currently without ACE. Predictions represent expected speed improvements based on route characteristics, baseline performance, and observed patterns from existing ACE deployments.

Study Limitations & Critical Considerations

Important: Understanding What This Model Can and Cannot Tell Us

This analysis represents rigorous data science work, but like all models, it has boundaries. Understanding these limitations is as important as understanding the findings themselves. A regression model is a powerful tool—but we must be cautious about letting any tool tell us the whole story.

The Circular Logic Challenge: Correlation vs. Causation

Critical Finding Requiring Nuance

Our model identifies "pre-ACE speed" as the strongest predictor (96.6% feature importance) of historical ACE route selection. This is a robust, well-documented correlation. However, we must be extremely careful about how we interpret this finding:

  • What we can say: Slower routes have historically been more likely to receive ACE implementation. This pattern is real and statistically significant.
  • What we cannot say: That pre-ACE speed is the only or even the most critical factor in actual deployment decisions. NYC DOT considers many factors beyond what our dataset captures.
  • The danger: Being "lured in by a good dataset" and forgetting that less quantifiable factors—community input, political feasibility, enforcement infrastructure readiness, street geometry, equity considerations—play crucial roles in real-world deployment decisions.

The Speed Improvement Fallacy

A critical caution about predictive modeling:

We observe that ACE routes experienced speed improvements. However, we must never commit the circular logic fallacy of saying "ACE routes saw speed improvements, and that's why we picked them to be ACE routes."

The Logic Error:

  • Pre-ACE speed can legitimately be a predictive factor for route selection (the cause)
  • Speed improvement is the effect of ACE implementation—it cannot logically drive the cause (route selection)
  • Past performance cannot predict future results: Aggregated/averaged ACE speed improvements from existing routes cannot be directly transferred to predict improvements on new routes

What would be needed for robust prediction: A detailed causal model incorporating expected ticketing volume, traffic studies of parking patterns, street infrastructure characteristics, pedestrian volume, signal timing, and route-specific congestion sources. Our model identifies candidates but cannot guarantee outcomes.

Data Limitations & Unmeasured Variables

What Our Dataset Cannot Capture

  • Enforcement Infrastructure: Camera installation costs, line-of-sight requirements, electrical infrastructure availability
  • Political & Community Context: Community board support, local political priorities, equity considerations across neighborhoods
  • Street Geometry: Lane width, turning radius constraints, physical space for bus lanes
  • Behavioral Factors: Driver adaptation to enforcement, spillover effects to adjacent streets
  • Economic Constraints: Budget availability, maintenance costs, enforcement personnel allocation
  • Operational Complexity: Interaction with truck delivery zones, construction schedules, special event impacts
  • Equity Analysis: Distribution of benefits across different demographic groups and neighborhoods

Model Assumptions & Their Implications

Our Ridge Regression model assumes:

  • Linear relationships between features and outcomes (reality is messier)
  • That historical patterns will continue to apply (policy priorities evolve)
  • That measured variables capture the decision-making process (they capture correlations, not necessarily causes)
  • That routes are comparable (each route has unique characteristics)
  • Stationarity of the system (traffic patterns, enforcement technology, and policy goals all change over time)

Appropriate Use of These Findings

✓ What This Analysis IS Good For

  • Identifying candidates for further investigation: Routes worth detailed traffic studies and community engagement
  • Prioritizing limited resources: Where to conduct expensive site assessments first
  • Pattern recognition: Understanding historical correlations in deployment decisions
  • Advocacy: Data-driven evidence to support student transportation needs
  • Starting conversations: Raising awareness about specific routes serving CUNY students

✗ What This Analysis Is NOT

  • A deployment decision tool: Cannot replace site-specific engineering studies
  • A guarantee of outcomes: Predicted improvements are estimates with substantial uncertainty
  • A complete picture of ACE effectiveness: Missing crucial implementation variables
  • A causal model: Identifies correlations, not proven cause-and-effect relationships
  • A substitute for domain expertise: Transportation planners have knowledge our data cannot capture

Recommendations for Future Research

Addressing Current Limitations

  • Causal inference methods: Implement propensity score matching, difference-in-differences, or instrumental variable approaches to move beyond correlation
  • Qualitative data integration: Conduct interviews with NYC DOT decision-makers to understand unmeasured selection criteria
  • Route-specific modeling: Develop individualized models incorporating street geometry, parking patterns, and local traffic conditions
  • Longitudinal validation: Track actual speed changes on newly implemented ACE routes to validate predictions
  • Equity analysis: Examine how ACE benefits are distributed across different neighborhoods and demographic groups
  • Cost-benefit analysis: Incorporate implementation costs and maintenance requirements into prioritization
  • Behavioral modeling: Study how drivers adapt to ACE enforcement over time and account for adaptation in predictions
